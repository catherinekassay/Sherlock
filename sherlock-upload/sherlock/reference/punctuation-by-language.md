# Punctuation by language: forensic reference

A reference for the translation-analyst subagent and the orthography skill. It helps decide what a non-English punctuation or number convention in an English text is **consistent with**.

**Sources**, all supplied by Kit on 26 September 2026 and reorganized here in our own words:
1. **"Punctuation of many languages,"** Polyglot Club wiki (revision 1 by GrimPixel). It cites Wikipedia's *Quotation mark* and *Decimal separator* articles, and is used for the multi-language tables.
2. **"French punctuation marks,"** FrenchPod101 blog (8 January 2026): frenchpod101.com/blog/2026/01/08/french-punctuation-marks/
3. **"Chinese punctuation,"** StoryLearning: storylearning.com/learn/chinese/chinese-tips/chinese-punctuation
4. **"Hindi punctuation,"** IndiaTyping: indiatyping.com/index.php/typing-tips/hindi-punctuation
5. **"Spanish punctuation,"** SpanishDict: spanishdict.com/guide/spanish-punctuation
6. **"The ultimate guide to German punctuation,"** Olesen Tuition: olesentuition.co.uk

Lines marked **[general knowledge]** are not from these sources.

**Reliability:** it's a community wiki page, and a few entries are unusual or overlap. For example, some countries appear under more than one decimal or grouping convention, and "·" is listed as a decimal separator for five places. Treat single entries as indicative, and verify any convention that decides a case.

## How to use this in a case

1. **A convention is evidence of a language or locale setting, not of a person's origin.** Keyboards, input methods, autocorrect, style guides, a translator's habits and machine translation all produce foreign conventions. Say "consistent with French typographic conventions," never "the writer is French."
2. **Check the software first.** Word, Google Docs and phone keyboards set to a language apply its quotes and spacing automatically. A **mix** of conventions in one text (for example «…» next to "…") is more telling than a consistent one: it can mean text pasted from different sources, partial editing, or a machine translation leaving source punctuation in place.
3. **Report the full list of languages a convention fits.** Many conventions are shared (for example, „…" is used from German to Lithuanian).
4. **Never infer nationality, ethnicity or immigration status** from punctuation.

## Language deep dives

### French (source 2)

| Convention | Detail | How it can show in English text |
|---|---|---|
| Space before two-part marks | A space goes before ? ! : ; (ideally a non-breaking space, so the mark doesn't wrap onto a new line). A semicolon has a space on both sides. | "Really ?" "Note : this" |
| Space before symbols | A space goes before %, currency signs and # | "15 %", "20 €" |
| Guillemets | « » for quotations, with a space inside each mark. Single ‹ › for quotations inside quotations. | « like this » |
| Introducing speech | A colon introduces a quotation: *Il a répondu : « D'accord ».* | "He answered : « OK »." |
| Dialogue | Longer dialogue gives each new speaker a line starting with a dash (tiret), not a new pair of quotation marks | Dialogue lines starting with "–" or "—" |
| No Oxford comma | *rouge, blanc et bleu* has no comma before "et" | No serial comma. Weak evidence: British English also usually omits it. |
| Decimal comma | *3,14* for 3.14 | "3,14" |
| Thousands | A thin space or a full stop: *1 000 000* or *1.000.000* | "1 000 000" |
| Currency | The symbol comes after the number, with a space: *10,00 €* | "10,00 €" |
| Apostrophe | Used only for elision (*l'ami, j'aime*), never for possession. Possession uses "de". | Possessives avoided ("the house of Marie") |
| Ellipsis | Always exactly three dots, and no extra full stop after it at the end of a sentence | "Je pense que…" style |
| Capitals | A capital after ? and !, as in English, but **no capital after a semicolon** | "; and so" |
| Accents | Required, including on capital letters in modern usage | Accented capitals (É, À) in names |
| Hyphens and parentheses | No spaces around hyphens. Parentheses follow English spacing. | |
| **Canadian French** | Usually **follows English spacing**, with no space before ? ! : ; | No space before marks in otherwise French-style text. This helps tell Quebec and France conventions apart. |

### Chinese (source 3)

| Mark | Name | Use | How it differs from English |
|---|---|---|---|
| ， | 逗号 dòuhào, comma | Pauses and separating clauses | Full-width. **Not used to separate list items** (see 、). |
| 、 | 顿号 dùnhào, enumeration comma | Separates list items, even in a two-item list, and can stand for "and". Also used after ordinal words: 第一、 ("First,") | No English equivalent |
| 。 | 句号 jùhào, full stop | Ends a sentence | A small circle that takes up a full character width |
| ？ ！ | Question and exclamation marks | Same as English | Full-width |
| ： ； | Colon and semicolon | Same as English | Full-width |
| （） | 括号, parentheses | Same as English | Full-width |
| " " and ' ' | 引号 yǐnhào, quotation marks (Simplified Chinese) | Speech and quotations. Single marks for quotations inside quotations. | Full-width curly quotes |
| 「」 and 『』 | Quotation marks (Traditional Chinese, Taiwan) | Double and nested quotations | Corner brackets. Rotated forms ﹁﹂﹃﹄ in vertical writing. |
| · | 间隔号 jiàngéhào, middle dot | Separates parts of foreign names (布兰妮·斯皮尔斯, "Britney Spears") and parts of dates (九·一一, "9/11") | No English equivalent |
| 《》 | 书名号 shūmínghào, title marks | Enclose titles of books, films and other works | English uses italics or quotation marks |
| …… | 省略号 shěnglüèhào, ellipsis | Same as English | **Six dots**, not three |

**Spacing and placement:**
- Every mark takes up one full character cell, and there are no spaces between words or after marks.
- Marks sit to the right of the character in horizontal writing, and below or to the right in vertical writing.
- **Pinyin text** uses the same marks, including 、, but a Western full stop, with Western capitalization. Apostrophes separate syllables where the reading could be ambiguous (Xī'ān).
- **[general knowledge]** The Chinese dash is a double-width ——.

### Hindi (source 4)

| Mark | Name | Use |
|---|---|---|
| । | पूर्ण विराम purna viram, full stop | Ends a complete sentence |
| ; | अर्धविराम ardha viram, semicolon | Joins related clauses without a conjunction |
| , | अल्प विराम alp viram, comma | Pauses, clauses, compound sentences, list items |
| : | उप विराम up viram, colon | Expands on what came before, often introducing a list |
| ! | विस्मयादिबोधक चिन्ह, exclamation mark | Surprise, exasperation, emphasis |
| ? | प्रश्नवाचक चिन्ह, question mark | Direct questions |
| " " | अवतरण or उद्धरण चिन्ह, quotation marks | Exact quotations and idiomatic expressions |
| :- | विवरण चिन्ह vivaran chinh, detail mark | Introduces details or a list |
| - | योजक चिन्ह yojak chinh, hyphen | Joins words |
| ..... | पदलोप चिन्ह padlop chinh, omission mark | Omitted text |
| _ | रेखांकन चिन्ह, underline mark | |
| ० | लाघव चिन्ह laaghav chinh, abbreviation mark | Marks abbreviations (for example, डॉ० for "Dr.") |
| ,, | पुनरुक्ति सूचक चिन्ह, repetition (ditto) mark | |
| S | दीर्घ उच्चारण चिन्ह, long-pronunciation mark | |
| ^ | विस्मरण or त्रुटिपूरक चिन्ह, omission / correction caret | Inserts something forgotten |
| ― | निर्देशक चिन्ह nirdeshak chinh, direction dash | Introduces a quotation or statement: *तुलसीदास ने सत्य कहा है ― "…"* |
| = | तुल्यता सूचक चिन्ह, equivalence mark | |
| () {} [] | कोष्ठक चिन्ह, brackets | |

**Observed in the source's examples:**
- a **space before ? and !** (*वाह ! … आना हुआ ?*)
- a space before the colon in lists (*नाम : मोहन, श्याम …*)

These spacing habits can carry into English written by Hindi writers. Note that, unlike French, they don't come with guillemets or a space before the semicolon.

### Spanish (source 5)

| Convention | Detail | How it can show in English |
|---|---|---|
| Inverted opening marks | ¿…? and ¡…! open the question or exclamation *where it starts*, even mid-sentence: *Eres el hermano de Laura, ¿verdad?*; *Si me vuelves a molestar, ¡te voy a acusar con mi mamá!* | ¿ ¡ in English text, or questions punctuated as tags: "You like grapes, right?" |

| No Oxford comma | *leche, manzanas y pan* | No serial comma. Weak evidence: British English also omits it. |
| Comma before a question tag | *Te gustan las uvas, ¿verdad?* | Heavy use of tag questions ("…, right?", "…, no?", "…, true?") |
| Clauses | A comma separates a dependent clause from the main clause: *Si no entiendes, no dudes en contactarme.* | |
| Times | *7.30* for 7:30 | "7.30". Weak evidence: also British. |
| Not covered by the source | [general knowledge] Dialogue introduced by a long dash (*raya*), with speech tags set off by dashes: *—Ven aquí —dijo ella—.* Lowercase days, months and nationalities. | Dialogue with — instead of quotation marks; "monday," "january," "spanish" in lowercase |

### German (source 6)

| Colon | Introduces speech, explanations and lists. The next word is **lowercase** unless it's a noun or the start of a quotation. | Lowercase full sentences after colons (weak evidence) |
| Quotation marks | Low-high „…"; the source says Swiss usage is "…". (Note: the multi-language table above lists «…» for Swiss German, so the sources conflict. Verify if it matters.) | „…" in English text |

## Other marks and uses

| Mark | Use and languages (from the source) |
|---|---|
| Space | English, French, German: between words. Thai, Lao: between sentences (not words). Vietnamese: between syllables. Chinese, Japanese: no spaces. |
| Enumeration comma 、 | Chinese: separating items in a list |
| Exclamation mark | English: exclamations and commands. German: exclamations and **with imperatives** (German style puts ! after many commands). |
| Wave dash ～ | Chinese, Japanese: number ranges (where English uses an en dash) |
| Angle brackets 《》 | Chinese: titles of books and works |
| Lenticular brackets 【】 | Japanese |
| Hexagonal brackets 〔〕 | Chinese: serial numbers of official documents, and the nationality or dynasty of an author |
| Interpunct · | Catalan: inside l·l to separate syllables. Occitan: to distinguish certain letter combinations. Japanese: separating parts of foreign names and list items (・). Chinese: separating parts of foreign names. |
| Musical note ♪ | Japanese: to show singing |

**[general knowledge], commonly seen in translated or second-language English:**
- **French:** puts a (thin) space before ? ! ; : as well as inside « ».
- **German:** often uses a comma before "dass" and "that"-type clauses, and before relative clauses, where English doesn't.
- **Spanish and Portuguese:** often use a dash (—) to introduce dialogue lines instead of quotation marks, as do French and Italian in fiction.
- **Russian:** commonly uses a dash (—) for dialogue and between subject and predicate ("Moscow — capital of Russia").
- **Dates:** day-first dates (26/09/2026, 26.09.2026) are common across Europe and most of the world. Year-first (2026-09-26 or 2026/09/26) is ISO style and common in East Asia.
