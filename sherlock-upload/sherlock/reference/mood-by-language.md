A reference for the translation-analyst subagent and the grammar skill. It explains how mood systems in other languages can show through in English written by second-language writers or produced by translation.

## The working theory, and how it was refined

**Kit's starting theory:** the subjunctive will be a giveaway, because English rarely uses it but languages like Spanish use it often.

**Refinement:**
1. **English barely has a subjunctive in its word forms.** The article describes it as a *clause type* that uses the bare verb ("Jill suggested that Paul **take** his medicine"), not a set of endings. So a Spanish speaker's subjunctive has almost nothing in English to attach to.
2. **What carries over is the structure around it.** Spanish uses *que + subjunctive* where English uses an infinitive or a different conjunction. Word-for-word, that gives "I want **that you come**" (*quiero que vengas*) and "before **that you go**" (*antes de que te vayas*). **These calques are the real giveaway.** [general knowledge: the calque patterns]
3. **They are not Spanish-only.** French (*que*), Italian and Portuguese (*que / che*), Romanian (*să*), German (*dass*) and Russian (*чтобы*) produce the same "want that" structure. Report it as consistent with that group of languages, never as "Spanish."
4. **The real English subjunctive points the other way.** Forms like "I insist that he **be** present" and "if I **were**" are **formal native usage**. The article calls them formal or archaic-sounding and largely replaced by the indicative. [general knowledge: they're more common in American than British English, where "should" is often used instead ("insist that he should be").] Their presence says little about a first language, and may suggest an educated native or formal register.
5. **Tense-mood mismatches are a second giveaway.** The article's own example of a common second-language error is "would" in *both* halves of a conditional ("I would buy it if I **would** earn more"). That matches languages that mark the conditional in both clauses.

## Mood systems summarized from the article

| Mood | What it does | Languages the article names | How it can show in English |
|---|---|---|---|
| **Indicative** | Facts and beliefs | All | |
| **Subjunctive** | Hypotheticals, wishes, emotions, opinions, polite requests, usually in dependent clauses | Romance (Spanish, French, Italian, Portuguese), German (two kinds: Konjunktiv I and II), Swedish (*vore*), Dutch, Danish, Russian, Ukrainian, Polish, Slovenian, Lithuanian, Irish, Hindi, Assamese | "que / dass / чтобы" calques (see below); rarely any English subjunctive form |
| **Conditional** | Events that depend on a condition | English uses "would + verb." Romance languages put the conditional in the *main* clause. **German, Finnish and Romanian use it in both clauses**, Irish marks both, Finnish and Polish repeat the marker, and in Dutch "zou" in the if-clause isn't wrong. Hindi's form matches the Romance perfect conditional. | **"If I would …, I would …"** |
| **Optative** | Hopes and wishes | Albanian, Ancient Greek, Hungarian, Kazakh, Japanese, Finnish, Nepali, Sanskrit | [general knowledge] Wish formulas with "may" ("May God bless you") in greetings and sign-offs |
| **Imperative** | Commands and requests | Nearly all. Romance languages have a first-person plural form (*Vayamos*, *Allons*). Hindi has present and future imperatives, each with intimate and formal forms. | [general knowledge] Politeness levels carried over ("Kindly do the needful"); future-form requests |
| **Jussive** | Commands to a third person | Latin and Hindi use the subjunctive for this. Arabic has separate jussive, subjunctive and imperative forms. | [general knowledge] "Let him come," "He should come" used as commands |
| **Potential** | Probability, "probably will" | Finnish (*-ne-*, mostly literary), Hungarian (*-hat / -het*, "may / can"), Japanese ("tentative"), Sanskrit, Sami. English uses may / can / must / ought. | Heavy or unusual use of "may / can" for permission and possibility |
| **Presumptive** | Presupposition, doubt, supposition ("must be doing") | Romanian (*oi face*), Hindi (*kartā hogā*: "you must / might be doing it"), Gujarati, Punjabi | [general knowledge] Indian English "He **will be** in the office now" or "He **must be** doing it" to mean *probably* |
| **Hypothetical** | Could have happened but didn't | A few languages | |
| **Inferential / evidential** | Reports events the speaker **didn't witness** | Bulgarian and other Balkan languages ("renarrative"), Turkish (*gitmiş* vs *gitti*), Estonian ("oblique") | [general knowledge] Frequent "apparently," "it seems," "they say" for unwitnessed events |
| **Interrogative** | A dedicated question mood | Welsh, Nenets, Eskimo languages, Greenlandic | |

## Language deep dives

### French (source 2)

- **Moods:** indicative (facts), subjunctive (doubt, emotion, desire, necessity), conditional (possibility and politeness), imperative (commands).
- **Subjunctive triggers:**
  - **necessity:** *il faut que, il est nécessaire / important que*
  - **desire:** *je veux que, je préfère que, j'aimerais que*
  - **emotion:** *je suis content que, c'est dommage que*
  - **doubt:** *je ne pense pas que, je doute que, il n'est pas certain que*
  - **possibility and judgment:** *il est possible / normal / étrange que*
  - **conjunctions:** *bien que, quoique* ("although"), *pour que, afin que* ("so that"), *avant que* ("before"), *à condition que* ("provided that"), *sans que* ("without")
- **The conditional covers four uses:**
  - politeness: *Je voudrais un café*
  - hypotheticals: *Si j'avais le temps, je voyagerais*. The if-clause uses the **imperfect, not the conditional**, so French speakers are *less* prone to "if I would."
  - advice: *Tu devrais te reposer*
  - **unconfirmed reports:** *Le ministre aurait démissionné*, "the minister has reportedly resigned"
- **Signs in English:**
  - "I want that you…," "before that you…," "for that you…," "without that he…," "it is necessary that I…"
  - **the reportative conditional:** "The minister **would have resigned**," meaning *reportedly*. This is a strong, distinctive calque.

### Spanish (source 3)

- **Moods:** indicative (facts), subjunctive (desires, doubts, wishes, conjectures, possibilities: *Es raro que John **haga** paella*), imperative (*haz la paella*).
- **Negative commands take the subjunctive:** *no **quemes** la cocina*.
- The source lists no further triggers. **[general knowledge]** The main triggers are wishes (*querer que*), emotion, impersonal judgments (*es importante que*), recommendations, doubt and denial, *ojalá*, and conjunctions (*para que, antes de que, sin que, aunque*, and *cuando* for future time).
- **Signs in English:** the calque table below. After "when" for future time, Spanish's subjunctive lines up with the English present tense ("when I arrive"), so Spanish speakers are **less** likely than French or Russian speakers to write "when I will arrive."

### German (sources 4, 5)

- **Moods:** indicative, imperative, and two subjunctives.
- **Imperative:**
  - three forms: *du* (*Mach die Hausaufgaben!*), *ihr* (*Macht…!*), *Sie* (*Machen Sie bitte…!*)
  - usually with "bitte" for politeness
- **Konjunktiv II** covers hypotheses, wishes, unreal situations, regrets and polite requests:
  - formed with *würde + infinitive* for most verbs
  - special forms for *haben / sein* (*hätte, wäre*) and the modals (*könnte, müsste, möchte, sollte, dürfte*)
  - past forms: *Wenn ich mich wärmer angezogen hätte, wäre ich nicht krank geworden.*
- **Konjunktiv I** is mainly for **reported speech** and keeping distance from the source: *Er sagte, er **habe** kein Interesse* ("he said he had no interest").
- **Signs in English:**
  - "would" in if-clauses: *würde* is used colloquially in both clauses
  - "I would gladly have…" politeness forms
  - reported speech with unexpected verb forms, or no shift of tense
  - "want that" (*dass*)
  - the comma habits in the punctuation reference ("I know, that…")

### Russian (sources 6, 7)

- **Conditional / subjunctive:**
  - *бы* + past tense
  - **the same form for present, past and future hypotheticals**
  - *бы* usually follows the verb
  - examples: *Если бы у меня было достаточно денег, я бы купил машину*; politeness: *Я хотел бы стакан воды*
- **Different-subject wishes and purpose:** *чтобы* + **past tense**: *Я хочу, чтобы ты признался мне во всём* ("I want you to confess everything to me").
- **Advice:** *Сходил бы ты к врачу* ("You'd better see a doctor"), literally "you would go to the doctor."
- **Imperatives:** formed from the stem (*думай / думайте, пиши / пишите, брось / бросьте*). **Let's**: *давай(те)*: *Давайте играть в шахматы*. **Third person**: *пусть / пускай*: *Пусть это служит примером* ("Let this serve as an example").
- **Signs in English:**
  - "I want that you **told** me everything" (a past tense after "want that")
  - tense confusion in hypotheticals ("would have" for present or future situations, or the reverse), because Russian doesn't mark time there
  - advice as "You would go to a doctor"
  - frequent "Let's…" and "Let him…" commands

### Danish (sources 8, 9)

- **Moods:** indicative and imperative, plus a rare optative in fixed expressions (*Gud være lovet!* "God be praised!"; *Kongen længe leve!* "Long live the king!").
- **No living subjunctive.** Like English, Danish expresses unreal situations by shifting the tense back (past for present unreal, pluperfect for past unreal). So **Danish speakers produce few mood calques.**
- **Imperative:**
  - the bare stem (*Kom her!*, *Kør langsomt!*)
  - normally without a subject, which goes after the verb if present
- **Features that do transfer to English:**
  - **no verb agreement with the subject**: "he go," "she have"
  - **the verb always comes second in main clauses (V2)**: "Yesterday went I to town"
  - **subordinate clauses put the adverb or "ikke" (not) before the verb**: "because he not came"
  - **the definite article is a suffix** (*bilen*, "the car"), which can lead to article errors
  - **the -s passive and the *blive* passive**: overuse of passives, and *blive* ("become") passives like "the house becomes painted"
- Danish modals take a bare infinitive, as English does.

## Interference patterns to look for in English

These are tendencies, not diagnoses. Quote each example, and report the languages it's consistent with. Several appear in native English too, so weigh them with the rest of the evidence.

| Pattern | Examples | Consistent with | Native lookalikes |
|---|---|---|---|
| **"want / hope / ask / tell … that + clause"** where English uses an infinitive | "I want that you come," "She asked that I help her" (as speech), "Tell him that he comes" | Spanish, French, Italian, Portuguese, Romanian (*să*), German (*dass*), Russian (*чтобы*) | "ask that" and "request that" are fine in formal English. "want that + clause" is not. |
| **"before / without / until that + clause"** | "before that you go," "without that he knows," "until that she arrives" | Spanish (*antes de que*, *sin que*, *hasta que*), French (*avant que*, *sans que*), Italian (*prima che*, *senza che*), Portuguese | None: native English drops "that" here |
| **"for that" meaning "so that"** | "I explain it for that you understand" | Spanish and Portuguese (*para que*), French (*pour que*), Italian (*perché*) | None |
| **"would" in the if-clause** | "If I would have more time, I would travel," "if he would come, we would start" | German, Dutch, Finnish, Romanian, Polish, Irish (all mark the conditional in both clauses) | **Common in informal American speech**, especially "if I would have known." Weak evidence on its own. |
| **Future after "when" or "if"** | "When I will arrive, I will call you" | [general knowledge] French (*quand j'arriverai*), Russian, Czech and others that use the future tense there | Rare in native writing |
| **Mood overcorrection** | English subjunctive forms used where natives use the indicative ("If he be late…") | Formal or archaic learning, or hypercorrection | Legal and very formal native style |
| **Reportative conditional** | "The minister **would have resigned**" (meaning *reportedly*) | French (*aurait démissionné*). [general knowledge] Also Italian and Portuguese. | None: native English uses "reportedly" or "is said to have" |
| **Past tense after "want that"** | "I want that you **told** me everything" | Russian (*чтобы* + past) | None |
| **"would" as advice** | "You would go to a doctor" (meaning "you should") | Russian (*Сходил бы ты к врачу*) | "I would see a doctor if I were you" is native. The bare second-person form isn't. |
| **Verb second with a pronoun subject after a fronted adverb** | "Yesterday went I to town," "Then said he" | Danish, German, Dutch, Swedish, Norwegian (verb-second languages) | Archaic or literary "Then came the rain" (noun subject) is native. Inversion with a *pronoun* subject is not. |
| **Adverb or "not" before the verb in a subordinate clause** | "because he not came," "that she always has been" | Danish and other Scandinavian languages | None |
| **Missing third-person -s** | "he go," "she have" | Danish, Swedish, Norwegian, Chinese and others without person agreement | Some native dialects, such as African American English. Report the variety, never ethnicity. |
| **Presumptive "will / must"** | "He will be at home now" (meaning *probably*) | Hindi, Punjabi, Gujarati (presumptive); Romanian | Native British "That'll be the postman" is similar. Weigh the frequency. |

## When mood transfer won't show

A **skilled human translator** writing into their native English removes these calques. A test on a hand-translated literary text found **no mood-transfer candidates at all**. Absence is **not** evidence that a text was originally written in English. The patterns in this file are most useful for:
- second-language writing
- literal translation
- machine translation

## How to use this with the scripts

The grammar script (`grammar_stats.py`) reports these in its mood section:
- **Mandative and "were" subjunctives:** likely native or formal
- **Subjunctive calque candidates:** "want that," "before that + pronoun," "for that + pronoun," and similar
- **"would" in if-clauses**

Every hit is a candidate. Read it in context: "before that, he left" is ordinary English, while "before that he leaves" is a calque.
